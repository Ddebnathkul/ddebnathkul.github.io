My passion for tropical plants

Aroids and beyond.

Growing up in a tropical country, this is a part of my home that I still can bring to my present living space. What I particularly love about aroids is the remarkable diversity of their foliage. Sometimes you see a new leaf pop up, and I think to myself how could nature devise something of such aweness and beauty. They can range from the velvety, deeply veined leaves of Anthuriums and Philodendrons to the fenestrated leaves of Monsteras, and the big string leaves of Alocasias (literally grows as weed in my home-town).

I enjoy watching plants develop from their juvenile to mature growth forms, and it gives me a sense of calmness and teaches me the fruits of passion, perseverance and patience. As a fellow science nerd I also geek about their natural habitats and growing conditions. My collection extends beyond aroids to begonias, tropical yams and other unusual foliage plants.

Growing medium

My soil mix.

For most of my aroids, I prefer a chunky, well-aerated mix that retains some moisture while allowing air to reach the roots. I adjust the components according to the plant and how quickly the pot dries out.

  • BarkCreates air pockets and gives the mix structure.
  • PerliteImproves aeration and drainage.
  • Coco coirRetains moisture without excessive compaction.
  • CharcoalImproves drainage, and controls odors.
  • LECAExpanded clay pebbles that provide a porous, inorganic growing medium.
  • PumiceA porous mineral component that improves aeration and drainage.

Alternatives: For moisture-loving begonias, I add vermiculite. For Alocasias, a semi-hydroponic mineral mix with pumice and zeolite is another option I favour.

Where I buy my supplies: I get my coco coir, perlite, LECA and pumice from AVEVE, and my charcoal from Sybotanica.

Climbing and maturation

Moss poles.

Many of the aroids I grow are natural climbers. Giving them a vertical support allows their aerial roots to attach and helps them develop larger, more mature foliage under suitable growing conditions.

  • SupportA sphagnum moss pole provides a rooting surface for aerial roots.
  • PlacementPosition the climbing side of the stem towards the pole.
  • MoistureKeep the moss lightly moist rather than constantly saturated.
  • GrowthExtend the pole as the climbing stem grows.

Climbing Philodendrons, Monsteras, Rhaphidophoras, Syngoniums and Epipremnums can particularly benefit from support. Not every aroid is a climber, so I adapt the setup to the plant's natural growth habit.

Where I buy my supplies: I order the sphagnum moss from Sybotanica, and the poles themselves from Amazon.

Monstera deliciosa ‘Thai Constellation’

Variegated cultivar

Creamy and marbled foliage with pale speckles and the characteristic fenestrations of mature Monstera leaves.

Family
Araceae
Native range
Southern Mexico to Guatemala (parent species).
Classification
A horticultural selection of M. deliciosa, propagated for its characteristic variegation.
Habitat
Warm, humid tropical forest; can mature while crawling and climbing.

Preferred growing conditions

Temperature
20–28 °C
Humidity (RH)
55–75%
Light
Bright, filtered light
Watering
Allow the upper mix to dry slightly; water thoroughly, then drain.

Philodendron ‘Splendid’

Interspecific hybrid

A velvety climbing philodendron with elongated heart-shaped leaves, contrasting veins and reddish tones on the undersides. It combines features of two striking rainforest species.

Family
Araceae
Native range
Parent species: P. verrucosum from Costa Rica to Peru; P. melanochrysum from Colombia.
Classification
Cultivated hybrid of P. verrucosum and P. melanochrysum. It has no natural native range of its own.
Habitat
Humid tropical forest-like conditions with good air circulation.

Preferred growing conditions

Temperature
20–27 °C
Humidity (RH)
65–80%
Light
Bright, indirect light
Watering
Keep lightly moist; avoid saturated or compacted soil.
Growing mix
Airy aroid mix with bark, perlite, coco coir, and charcoal. To mature the plant it needs a moss pole or other climbing support.

Anthurium warocqueanum

Botanical species

The Queen Anthurium is prized for its elongated, dark velvety leaves and pale contrasting veins. Its dramatic mature leaves reward stable, humid growing conditions. Not a plant for beginner plant parents, is extremely fussy and loves stable conditions. Some species grows on higher elevation, thus tolerates changes, however the varieties growing in lower ranges close to the river will need a temperature and humidity controlled environment.

Family
Araceae
Native range
Colombia.
Classification
Naturally occurring species.
Habitat
Warm, humid tropical rainforest conditions.

Preferred growing conditions

Temperature
20–27 °C
Humidity (RH)
70–85%
Light
Bright, indirect light
Watering
Loves staying lightly moist; avoid drying and waterlogging.
Growing mix
Airy aroid mix with bark, perlite, coco coir, and charcoal; the new leaves are extremely fragile, thus allow space for leaves to hang freely; a climbing support such as moss pole may be necessary as the plant matures.

Aglaonema pictum ‘Tricolor’

Selected foliage form

An exceptionally patterned Chinese evergreen with camouflage-like patches in several shades of green. Not the easiest Aglaonema for a new plant parent.

Family
Araceae
Native range
Sumatra, including Nias, Indonesia (species range).
Classification
A three-coloured form of A. pictum, commonly sold as Tricolor. Kew treats the historical name A. pictum var. tricolor as a synonym of A. pictum, rather than a separate accepted variety.
Habitat
Airy aroid mix with bark, perlite, coco coir, and charcoal; avoid cold drafts.

Preferred growing conditions

Temperature
22–28 °C
Humidity (RH)
65–80%
Light
Soft, filtered light
Watering
Water when the upper mix begins to dry, maintaining gentle moisture.
Growing mix
Airy, moisture-retentive substrate; avoid compacted or cold, wet soil.
